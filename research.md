<article class="paper">
  <h2 class="paper__title">Demographic Origins of Premature Deindustrialization</h2>
  <div class="paper__links"><span class="paper__note">Draft coming soon!</span></div>
  <p class="paper__presented"><strong>Presented:</strong> RCEA Conference 2026; XXIX Workshop on Dynamic Macroeconomics 2026; RES Annual Conference 2026; Economics JM Bootcamp 2026; EEA-ESEM Congress 2026; Columbia Business School Macro Lunch; IMF-OECD-PIIE-WB Conference on Labor Markets, AI, and Demography 2026 (forthcoming); European Winter Meeting of the Econometric Society (EWMES) 2026 (forthcoming); CEPR Paris Symposium 2026 (forthcoming); Simposio de la Asociación Española de Economía (SAEe) 2026 (forthcoming); STEG Theme 3 Workshop 2027 (forthcoming).</p>
  <details class="abstract">
    <summary>Abstract</summary>
    <p class="abstract__body">The world is aging rapidly. Many developing economies have older workforces than today's high-income countries had at comparable stages of structural transformation. This paper asks whether workforce aging contributes to premature deindustrialization—manufacturing's share of employment peaking at lower levels and lower incomes—and to slower output growth. I document a pronounced age decline in manufacturing's share of employment within cohorts, using census data from 88 countries; this shift from manufacturing toward services is also evident within individual careers in Indonesian panel data. Life-cycle changes in workers' productive abilities provide an explanation. Manufacturing relies more on physical and fluid-cognitive abilities, which peak earlier in life than the crystallized knowledge used more intensively in services. I construct sectoral productivity curves using age–ability profiles from studies of aging, weighted by sector ability requirements. These curves closely match the direction and shape of the documented sectoral sorting by age. I then develop an open-economy model with a tractable theory of life-cycle worker sorting, calibrated to Indonesia over 1976–2025. Holding all its non-demographic features fixed, I solve the model under the younger demographic path of the historical United States and the older path of Thailand, a rapidly aging late industrializer, aligning the paths by the stage of structural transformation. Under the younger path, the peak manufacturing employment share is 4.8 percentage points higher and cumulative real output-per-worker growth is 19.5 percentage points higher. More restrictive export conditions reduce the gap in peak manufacturing employment shares by one third.</p>
  </details>
</article>

  <p class="paper__presented"><strong>Presented:</strong> BdI-ECB Trade, Value Chains and Financial Linkages in the Global Economy Conference 2026 (forthcoming); BdF-BoE-BdI International Macroeconomics Workshop 2026 (forthcoming).</p>
  <details class="abstract">
    <summary>Abstract</summary>
    <p class="abstract__body">This paper explores the macroeconomic consequences of levying tariffs on imported investment goods, which directly affect the household’s investment Euler equation. First, we construct a new multi-country and multi-sector investment input-output matrix to trace investment goods through international production chains. Second, we embed this in an open-economy New Keynesian model with production networks. In a uniform US tariff experiment, this channel more than doubles the impact contraction in GDP, with investment-goods exposure as the best predictor of aggregate output losses. Holding the average tariff fixed, redesigning its composition to avoid the investment network cuts cumulative domestic output losses by two-thirds.</p>

<article class="paper">
  <h2 class="paper__title">Trade, Financial Frictions, and the Missing Manufacturing Window</h2>
  <p class="paper__authors">with Santiago Etchegaray</p>
  <p class="paper__status">Submitted</p>
  <div class="paper__links">
    <a href="/files/Paper_STFF_MDJ_SE.pdf?v=20260609" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Draft</a>
    <a href="https://ssrn.com/abstract=7066615" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">SSRN</a>
    <a href="/files/STFF_CEMFI_WP_2607.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">CEMFI WP version</a>
    <a href="/files/Slides_STFF_MDJ_SE.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Slides</a>
  </div>
  <p class="paper__presented"><strong>Presented:</strong> Simposio de la Asociación Española de Economía (SAEe) 2024.</p>
  <details class="abstract">
    <summary>Abstract</summary>
    <p class="abstract__body">Why do some economies experience a pronounced manufacturing phase during structural transformation, while others move more directly into low-skilled services? This paper shows that financial underdevelopment, by shaping export competitiveness and domestic investment demand, is a quantitatively important driver of flat-manufacturing paths. Motivating evidence links financial depth to manufacturing activity and export performance. We then quantify the mechanism in a dynamic multi-country model of structural transformation and trade, where financial underdevelopment both weakens competitiveness in finance-dependent sectors and lowers demand for manufacturing-intensive investment goods. Moving flat-manufacturing economies halfway to the financial frontier closes over a quarter of the observed flat–steep peak gap; it raises real output per worker by 13 to 17 percent and real consumption per worker by 8 to 12 percent. Paired with lower nonfinancial trade costs, the same financial improvement closes almost three quarters of the peak gap, as finance shapes the manufacturing response that openness amplifies.</p>
  </details>
</article>

<article class="paper">
  <h2 class="paper__title">IMF Governance in a Fragmenting World</h2>
  <p class="paper__authors">with <a href="https://sites.google.com/view/mgeiras/inicio?authuser=0" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Martín Gonzalez-Eiras</a></p>
  <p class="paper__note">Draft available upon request</p>
  <details class="abstract">
    <summary>Abstract</summary>
    <p class="abstract__body">We develop a welfare-based theory of IMF governance in which voting weights and contributions reflect economic size, crisis risk, and trade linkages that transmit crises across countries. In the relatively balanced risk environment of Bretton Woods, the efficient voting rule can be implemented by quotas proportional to GDP at PPP and openness. After Bretton Woods, as IMF borrowing shifted toward emerging and developing economies, quotas remain optimal, but vote aggregation must account for unequal crisis risk. In a bipolar world with alliance-dependent trade costs, the model’s efficient allocation gives more voice to China and countries exposed to China-centered trade. Without governance reform, a China-centered coalition may find the Fund less attractive, raising the risk of institutional separation.</p>
  </details>
</article>
